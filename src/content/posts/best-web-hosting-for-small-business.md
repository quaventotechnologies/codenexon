## How this shortlist was put together

We started from the question a small business owner actually asks: what will this cost me, what do I get, and what could go wrong?

**What we compared**

- First-term price, the term required to get it, and the renewal price.
- Total cost over five years, calculated from those published rates.
- Number of websites, storage and any stated traffic guidance.
- Backup frequency and whether restores are mentioned.

## What a small business website needs from a host

Before comparing plans, be clear about what you are buying. A small business site needs six things.

1. **To stay online.** Look for a stated uptime commitment and a public status page.
2. **To load quickly for your customers.** That depends on server location and caching more than on plan size.
3. **To be recoverable.** Automatic backups, and a way to restore one without paying a surprise fee.
4. **To be secure.** Free HTTPS, current software versions and, ideally, malware scanning.
5. **To have help available.** Support you can reach during your working hours.
6. **To have a predictable bill.** A renewal price you knew about before you signed up.

Notice what is not on the list: unlimited bandwidth, dozens of email accounts, a website builder. Those appear on every plan card and rarely decide whether a host is right for you.

## When shared hosting is not the right fit

### A VPS: fixed resources, flat price, your responsibility

A virtual private server gives you reserved CPU and memory and full control. Pricing is flat, with no promotional rate and no renewal jump.

| Provider | Monthly price | Memory | vCPUs | SSD | Transfer |
|----------|---------------|--------|-------|-----|----------|
| DigitalOcean | $4 | 512 MiB | 1 | 10 GB | 500 GiB |
| DigitalOcean | $6 | 1 GiB | 1 | 25 GB | 1,000 GiB |
| DigitalOcean | $12 | 2 GiB | 1 | 50 GB | 2,000 GiB |
| Amazon Lightsail | $5 | 0.5 GB | 2 | 20 GB | 1 TB |
| Amazon Lightsail | $7 | 1 GB | 2 | 40 GB | 2 TB |
| Amazon Lightsail | $12 | 2 GB | 2 | 60 GB | 3 TB |

A $6 server costs $360 over five years, which is below every shared plan above except Hostinger Premium. The catch is the work. You install the web server, apply security updates, configure the firewall, set up backups and fix it when it breaks. For a business owner without technical help, that is not a saving.

Consider a VPS when you have someone technical, need software a shared host will not run, or host several sites.

### A developer platform: for sites built with code

If your site is built with a framework such as Next.js, Astro or SvelteKit, a developer platform deploys it straight from your code repository and serves it from a global network.

## Buyer's guide: how to choose in five steps

### Step 1: Work out the real cost

Take the first-term price, multiply by the term, then add the renewal price for the remaining months of the period you care about. Three years is a sensible horizon for a new business. Compare the effective monthly figures, not the headline ones.

### Step 2: Decide how long you will commit

Long terms are cheaper per month and cost more on day one. Paying $143 for three or four years makes sense for an established business. For an untested idea, a 12-month term limits your risk even though the monthly rate is higher.

### Step 3: Check the three things that protect you

- **Backups.** Daily is the standard to aim for. Weekly is acceptable for a site that rarely changes. Confirm that restoring is free.
- **Refund window.** All three shared hosts here list 30 days. Domain fees are usually excluded.
- **Uptime terms.** A 99.9% guarantee allows about 8 hours 46 minutes of downtime a year. A 99.99% guarantee allows about 53 minutes. Read what compensation is offered.

### Step 4: Match the limits to your next year

Count the sites you will run and estimate your traffic. A new business site with a few hundred visits a month fits any entry plan here. Do not pay for the next five years of growth today. Upgrading within a host is quick.

### Step 5: Test during the refund period

This is where you find out what the plan page cannot tell you.

1. Install your site, or a copy of it, in the first week.
2. Measure Time to First Byte from your customers' region. Google's guidance is 0.8 seconds or less. [What Is TTFB?](/what-is-ttfb) shows how.
3. Open a support chat with a real question and time the response.
4. Take a backup and restore it.
5. If anything disappoints, ask for the refund before day 30.

## Ten questions to ask a host before you pay

Plan pages leave gaps. Open the host's pre-sales chat and ask these directly. The answers, and how quickly and clearly you get them, tell you a lot.

1. What is the renewal price for this plan, and does it change with the renewal term I pick?
2. How often are backups taken, how long are they kept, and is restoring one free?
3. Which data center locations can I choose from?
4. Is there a written uptime commitment, and what do I receive if it is missed?
5. What are the limits on CPU, memory and number of files for this plan?
6. Which PHP versions are available, and can I change version myself?
7. Is email included after the first year, and what does it cost if not?
8. What does the "free" domain renew at in year two?
9. Is there a staging area for testing changes?
10. If I cancel inside the refund window, what is deducted?

Save the chat transcript. If the answer to a question later turns out to be wrong, you have it in writing.

Three of these matter more than the rest for a business. The renewal price decides your long-term cost. The backup answer decides how bad your worst day can be. The limits answer decides how soon you will be asked to upgrade. A host that answers all three plainly is usually a host that is straightforward to deal with later.

## Signs you have outgrown your plan

Do not upgrade on a hunch. These are the signals worth acting on.

- Your host emails you about CPU or memory limits.
- Time to First Byte on cached pages is regularly above 0.8 seconds.
- The site slows down or errors during your normal busy hours.
- The WordPress dashboard takes several seconds to load each screen.
- You are running out of storage or have reached the plan's site limit.

Before paying more, rule out the cheap fixes: page caching, compressed images and a current PHP version. [Why Is My WordPress Site Slow?](/why-is-my-wordpress-site-slow) lists twelve in order, and [Core Web Vitals Explained](/core-web-vitals-explained) shows how to measure whether they worked.

## The setup around your hosting

Hosting is one part of getting a business online. These guides cover the rest.

### Your domain and DNS

Your domain is your address, and DNS records connect it to your host and your email. A handful of record types do all the work. Registering the domain separately from your hosting keeps a future move simple. See [DNS Records Explained: A, CNAME, MX and TXT in Plain English](/dns-records-explained).

### HTTPS

Every business site needs an SSL certificate, and it should be free. All three shared hosts above include one. If you run your own server, [How to Get a Free SSL Certificate With Let's Encrypt](/free-ssl-certificate-lets-encrypt) has the commands.

### Speed for far-away customers

If your customers are spread across countries, a content delivery network serves your files from a location near each of them. Many hosts include one. [What Is a CDN and Does a Small Website Need One?](/what-is-a-cdn) explains when it matters.

### Business email that reaches the inbox

Whether your mailboxes come with your hosting or from a separate provider, your domain needs three DNS records so that invoices and replies are not treated as spam. [SPF, DKIM and DMARC Explained](/spf-dkim-dmarc-explained) lists them.

### Keeping WordPress healthy

Most small business sites run on WordPress. Three guides cover the essentials: [backups you can restore](/how-to-back-up-wordpress-site), [fixing a slow site](/why-is-my-wordpress-site-slow) and [moving to a new host](/how-to-migrate-wordpress-site).

### Taking payments

If you sell online, your payment processor will cost far more than your hosting. On $10,000 of monthly sales, fees run from about $347 to $447 depending on the provider and product. See [Stripe vs PayPal Fees](/stripe-vs-paypal-fees).

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best web hosting for a small business?

For most small businesses, an entry shared plan from an established host is enough. On October 2026 pricing, Hostinger Premium has the lowest five-year cost at $275.40, Bluehost Starter includes 10 websites and staging for $383.40, and SiteGround StartUp has the shortest commitment but costs $911.40 over five years.

### How much does small business web hosting cost?

Entry shared hosting costs $2.99 to $3.99 a month during the first term, paid in advance for 12 to 48 months. At renewal the same plans cost $9.99 to $17.99 a month. Budget on the renewal figure, since that is what you pay for most of the site's life.

### Is shared hosting good enough for a business website?

Yes, for a brochure site, a blog or a small store with modest traffic. Bluehost says its Starter plan suits about 40,000 visits a month, and SiteGround says StartUp suits about 10,000. Move up when you see resource warnings or slow pages that caching does not fix.

### Should a small business use a VPS?

Only if someone technical will manage it. A VPS from DigitalOcean starts at $4 to $6 a month with no renewal increase, but you handle updates, security and backups yourself. Without that skill in the business, shared or managed hosting is the safer choice.

### Do I need to buy the longest hosting term?

No. The longest term gives the lowest monthly rate and delays the renewal increase, but you pay it all at once. Choose it when you are confident in the business and the host. Otherwise a 12-month term costs more per month and keeps you flexible.

### Which hosting includes email?

On October 6, 2026, Hostinger listed 2 mailboxes per website on Premium and unlimited on its Unlimited plan, free for the first year. Bluehost's Starter and Business plan cards did not list email as included. Check what email costs after any free period.

### How do I know if a host is fast before committing?

Use the refund window. All three shared hosts here list a 30-day money-back guarantee. Install your site, measure Time to First Byte from your customers' region and aim for 0.8 seconds or less. If the result disappoints, request a refund within the 30 days.

### Can I change hosts later?

Yes. A website is a set of files and a database, and both can be copied to a new host. With a lowered DNS TTL and a test on the new server before switching, visitors should not notice the move. Our WordPress migration guide covers each step.

## Glossary

- **Shared hosting:** many websites on one server, sharing its resources.
- **VPS:** a virtual private server with reserved CPU and memory that you manage.
- **Renewal price:** the regular rate charged after the promotional first term ends.
- **Uptime SLA:** a written commitment to a percentage of time the service will be available.
- **TTFB:** Time to First Byte, the wait before a server starts responding.
- **CDN:** content delivery network, which serves files from locations near visitors.
- **DNS:** the system that connects a domain name to a server.
- **SSL certificate:** what enables HTTPS, the encrypted connection to your site.
- **Staging site:** a private copy of your site for testing changes.
