How to Protect Yourself and Your Business From Ransomware

Ransomware attacks now routinely combine file encryption with data theft. Here's exactly how attacks typically get in, the specific defenses that work, and what to do in the first hours if you're already hit.

Shubham Handore Published 13 Sep, 2026 ~12 min read

Ransomware has evolved from simple file-locking malware into a professionalized criminal industry that also steals your data before encrypting it. This guide covers how attacks actually happen and the concrete steps that meaningfully reduce your risk.

Quick answer: Reduce ransomware risk by keeping offline, tested backups, patching internet-facing systems promptly, enforcing multi-factor authentication on remote access, and training people to spot phishing, and if you're already hit, isolate affected systems immediately and avoid paying as a first response.

What Is Ransomware, Exactly?

Ransomware is malicious software that encrypts your files, or locks you out of your systems entirely, and then demands payment, usually in cryptocurrency, in exchange for a decryption key. Modern ransomware attacks frequently go a step further with "double extortion": before encrypting anything, the attacker quietly copies your sensitive data, then threatens to publish it publicly if you don't pay, even if you're able to restore your files from backup without their help. This means backups alone, while essential, no longer fully neutralize the threat the way they once did.

The impact goes well beyond the ransom demand itself. Organizations hit by ransomware routinely report weeks of operational downtime, the cost of forensic investigation and system rebuilding, regulatory penalties if customer or patient data was exposed, and lasting reputational damage, costs that in many documented cases add up to several times the ransom amount, even when the ransom is never paid at all.

Ransomware isn't a single piece of software, it's a category, with dozens of active variants operated by different criminal groups, many of whom now run "ransomware-as-a-service" operations that rent out their tools to other attackers for a cut of the ransom. This professionalization is a big part of why ransomware attacks have become more frequent and more damaging over the past several years, rather than declining as awareness of the threat has grown.

The ransomware-as-a-service model is worth understanding because it explains why the threat looks the way it does today: the people who write the encryption malware, the people who break into networks, and the people who negotiate ransoms are often entirely separate groups working together for a share of the payout. This division of labor means an attack can be professional and well-executed even when carried out by someone with relatively limited technical skill of their own, simply renting sophisticated tools built by someone else.

How Ransomware Actually Gets In

Despite the sophistication of the criminal groups behind it, ransomware overwhelmingly gets onto a system through a small number of well-understood paths:

  • Phishing emails remain the single most common entry point, a malicious attachment or a link to a fake login page that harvests credentials, which are then used to access systems directly.
  • Exposed remote access services, particularly Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) endpoints left open to the internet with weak or reused passwords, are a favorite target for automated scanning tools that attackers run constantly looking for easy entry points.
  • Unpatched software vulnerabilities in internet-facing systems (VPN appliances, web servers, and other network infrastructure) let attackers in without needing any credentials at all, which is why patching remains one of the highest-use defenses available.
  • Compromised third-party vendors or software supply chains can introduce ransomware indirectly, a trusted piece of software or a managed service provider gets breached first, and the attacker uses that access to reach every client downstream.

Once inside, attackers typically don't encrypt anything immediately. They spend time moving laterally through the network, identifying backup systems (to disable or corrupt them first), and locating the most valuable data to steal before triggering encryption, which is why the visible "attack" is often just the final stage of a process that began days or weeks earlier. Security researchers analyzing real incidents commonly find attackers had access to a network for anywhere from several days to several weeks before the encryption event, quietly exploring and preparing, which is also why detecting unusual activity early, before encryption starts, is one of the most valuable things a monitoring system can do.

Protecting Yourself as an Individual

For personal devices, a handful of habits cover most of the realistic risk:

  • Keep automatic backups running to a location that isn't permanently connected to your main device, an external drive you disconnect after backing up, or a cloud backup service with file versioning, so a compromised device can't also encrypt or delete your backup copies.
  • Turn on automatic operating system and software updates. Many ransomware infections exploit vulnerabilities that were already patched months earlier, the victims simply hadn't installed the update yet.
  • Be skeptical of unexpected attachments and links, even ones that appear to come from someone you know, since compromised accounts are often used to send convincing phishing messages to their contacts.
  • Use unique, strong passwords with a password manager, and enable multi-factor authentication wherever it's offered, particularly for email, since email access is often the key that enables resets for everything else.
  • Follow the 3-2-1 backup rule where practical: three copies of important data, on two different types of media, with one copy stored offsite or offline, a pattern originally developed for disaster recovery in general that happens to hold up very well against ransomware specifically.

None of these habits require deep technical expertise or significant ongoing cost, the biggest barrier for most individuals is simply following through consistently rather than treating backups and updates as a one-time setup task. Setting a recurring reminder to verify your backups are actually running, rather than assuming they are, closes one of the most common gaps between "we have a backup policy" and "we have a backup that actually works when needed."

Protecting a Business from Ransomware

Businesses face a larger attack surface and higher stakes, so defenses need to go further than individual good habits:

  • Maintain offline, immutable backups that an attacker with full network access still can't reach or modify, the single most important defense against being forced to pay, since it removes the use of "pay or lose your data" (though it does nothing against the data-theft half of a double-extortion attack).
  • Segment the network so that a compromise in one part of the business (a single employee's laptop, for instance) can't spread freely to every server and system, limiting how much damage one initial foothold can cause.
  • Enforce multi-factor authentication on all remote access, especially VPN and RDP, and close off RDP from the open internet entirely wherever possible, relying on a VPN or other controlled access method instead.
  • Patch internet-facing systems promptly, prioritizing anything exposed directly to the internet, since those are what automated attacker scans find first.
  • Run regular phishing-awareness training for employees, since human error in clicking a malicious link or attachment remains one of the most common initial entry points regardless of how strong the technical defenses are.
  • Have a written, tested incident response plan that specifies who does what during an active attack, figuring this out for the first time while systems are actively being encrypted is a recipe for costly delays and mistakes.
  • Deploy endpoint detection and response (EDR) tooling on servers and workstations, which can flag the unusual behavior patterns (mass file encryption, credential-dumping tools, suspicious lateral movement) that precede a full-blown ransomware event, giving defenders a chance to intervene before the damage is done.
  • Test backup restoration regularly, not just backup creation, a backup that turns out to be corrupted or incomplete only becomes apparent, for many organizations, at the worst possible moment: during an actual recovery attempt.

Larger organizations increasingly formalize this into a broader practice sometimes called "cyber resilience", the assumption that a serious incident will eventually happen despite good defenses, with planning focused on minimizing damage and recovery time rather than assuming prevention alone is sufficient.

What to Do If You're Already Hit

If ransomware has already activated on your systems, the sequence of actions matters, and panic-driven decisions in the first hour are one of the most common ways an already-bad situation gets worse. A calm, methodical response, even under real pressure, gives you a meaningfully better outcome than an improvised one:

  1. Isolate affected systems immediately by disconnecting them from the network, this can stop the encryption from spreading further to systems it hasn't reached yet.
  2. Do not pay the ransom as a first response. Paying doesn't guarantee you'll get a working decryption key, doesn't undo any data theft that already happened, and funds further criminal activity. Law enforcement agencies in most countries recommend against paying, and many organizations that do pay still lose data or get hit again.
  3. Contact law enforcement and, if you have one, your cyber insurance provider before making any other major decisions, insurers often have specific incident-response requirements and vetted recovery partners.
  4. Bring in an incident response specialist if you don't have in-house expertise, determining the scope of the breach and safely restoring systems is genuinely specialized work, and doing it wrong can leave a backdoor the attacker still controls even after you think you've recovered.
  5. Restore from clean, verified backups only after the initial point of entry has been identified and closed, restoring onto a still-compromised network just invites a repeat attack.

Should You Ever Pay the Ransom?

This is a genuinely difficult decision that organizations sometimes face despite every recommendation against it, usually because backups have failed, are incomplete, or the stolen data is sensitive enough that the extortion threat alone is unacceptable. There's no universally correct answer, it depends on the specific situation, legal obligations, and whether continuing operations without the encrypted data is realistically possible. What's clear is that this decision should never be made in a panic during the first hours of an attack; it should follow legal counsel, law enforcement guidance, and a clear-eyed assessment of what paying would and wouldn't actually solve.

A few sobering realities are worth weighing carefully before considering payment. First, paying marks your organization as a demonstrated payer, which can invite repeat targeting, either from the same group or others who buy or share victim lists. Second, in some jurisdictions, paying a ransom to a sanctioned entity or group can itself carry legal risk, independent of the ransomware attack, which is exactly why involving legal counsel before any payment decision is not optional. Third, even a "successful" payment that restores files does nothing to address a data-theft component of the attack, there is no reliable way to verify a criminal group has actually deleted stolen data rather than keeping or reselling it regardless of what they claim.

Cyber Insurance and Ransomware

Cyber insurance has become a meaningful part of ransomware preparedness for many businesses, covering costs like incident response, legal fees, notification requirements, and in some cases the ransom itself. However, insurers have tightened requirements considerably in recent years in response to the rising frequency of claims, policies increasingly require specific controls (multi-factor authentication, offline backups, endpoint detection tools) as a condition of coverage, and a business that can't demonstrate those controls may find it harder to get a policy at all, or may face a claim denial after an incident if the required controls turn out not to have been in place.

It's worth treating the insurance application process itself as a useful security audit: the specific controls insurers ask about are, in practice, a reasonably good proxy for the defenses that actually reduce ransomware risk, drawn from patterns insurers have observed across large numbers of real claims. A business that struggles to answer those questions honestly has usually just identified its own biggest gaps, insurance aside.

How Attackers Choose Their Targets

Ransomware operators generally aren't hand-picking victims based on who they'd most like to attack, the large majority of attacks start with automated, opportunistic scanning across huge swaths of the internet, looking for known vulnerabilities, exposed remote access, or weak credentials. Once a scan finds a way in, a human operator typically takes over to assess how valuable the target looks (based on company size, apparent revenue, and how critical the encrypted systems seem to be) before deciding how much ransom to demand.

This is an important point for smaller organizations that assume they're "too small to be a target." Attackers running automated scans don't know or care how big you are until after they're already inside, by which point the cost of the attack has already been incurred, regardless of company size. Larger, well-known organizations do face more targeted attacks in addition to this opportunistic background noise, but the baseline risk from automated scanning applies to essentially anyone with an internet-facing system.

Certain sectors do see more deliberate targeting than others, healthcare, education, local government, and critical infrastructure have all been disproportionately targeted in recent years, likely because outages in these sectors create urgent pressure to restore service quickly, which attackers correctly expect increases the odds of a ransom being paid. Organizations in these sectors should treat that improved targeting as a real factor in how much they invest in defense, not just a general industry talking point.

Common Ransomware Myths Worth Retiring

  • "We're too small to be worth attacking." As covered above, most attacks are opportunistic and automated, not a deliberate choice to target a specific small business.
  • "Antivirus software alone will stop it." Modern endpoint protection helps, but ransomware operators actively test their tools against popular antivirus products before deploying them, and no single tool is a complete defense on its own, layered defenses matter more than any one product.
  • "Paying the ransom is the fastest, cheapest way to recover." Recovery costs (downtime, forensic investigation, rebuilding trust, potential regulatory penalties for a data breach) frequently dwarf the ransom itself, and paying doesn't address any of those costs, it only addresses the encryption, and only if the decryption tool provided actually works.
  • "Mac and Linux systems aren't targeted." While the large majority of ransomware is built for Windows, ransomware targeting other operating systems and cloud infrastructure directly does exist and has grown as attackers follow where valuable data increasingly lives.
  • "Once we're back up and running, the incident is over." Restoring systems addresses the immediate operational disruption, but data-breach notification obligations, regulatory scrutiny, customer trust rebuilding, and the risk of the same vulnerability being exploited again if it wasn't actually fixed can all extend well beyond the visible recovery.

Retiring these assumptions matters because each one tends to lead directly to under-investment in a specific, concrete defense, believing you're "too small to target" discourages basic hardening, and believing antivirus alone is sufficient discourages the layered approach that actually works.

Building a Realistic Ransomware Defense Posture

No single control on this list is sufficient by itself, and that's intentional, ransomware defense works on the same principle as most serious security practice: layered defenses, where each control catches what the previous one might have missed. Backups protect you if prevention fails; network segmentation limits the damage if backups and prevention both fail; an incident response plan limits the cost if segmentation doesn't fully contain an attack. The goal isn't an impossible guarantee of never being attacked, it's making sure that if an attack does get through one layer, it doesn't automatically get through all of them.

For most individuals and small businesses, the realistic starting point is the handful of controls repeated throughout this guide: offline backups, prompt patching, multi-factor authentication, and basic phishing awareness. These aren't exotic or expensive measures, and together they close off the large majority of the attack paths that account for most real-world ransomware incidents. Treat this as an ongoing practice rather than a one-time project, attackers adapt their methods over time, and a defense posture that was solid a year ago can quietly fall behind if it isn't periodically revisited, tested, and updated as new threats and vulnerabilities emerge.

FAQs

Will paying the ransom guarantee I get my files back?
No. Some victims who pay never receive a working decryption key, or receive one that only partially recovers their data. Law enforcement in most countries recommends against paying for this reason, among others.

Do backups alone protect against modern ransomware?
Not completely. Backups protect against losing your files, but many modern attacks steal data before encrypting it and threaten to leak it regardless of whether you restore from backup, this is called double extortion.

What's the single most effective defense against ransomware?
There isn't one silver bullet, but offline/immutable backups combined with prompt patching of internet-facing systems and multi-factor authentication on remote access cover the large majority of realistic attack paths.

Is Remote Desktop Protocol (RDP) safe to expose to the internet?
Generally no. Open RDP ports are one of the most commonly scanned and exploited entry points for ransomware. Access it only through a VPN or other controlled method, not directly from the open internet.

Should a small business worry about ransomware, or is it just a big-company problem?
Small businesses are frequently targeted precisely because they often have weaker defenses and less capacity to negotiate or recover, making the potential payout-to-effort ratio attractive to attackers running automated, high-volume campaigns.
